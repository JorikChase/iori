## Q1. Sohl-Dickstein 2024: what exactly is fractal, at what dimensions, for which networks?

### Takeaway
The fractal object is the boundary, in a 2-D plane of hyperparameters (mostly the two per-layer learning rates of a one-hidden-layer, width-16 MLP trained by full-batch gradient descent), between runs that converge and runs that diverge. Box-counting dimensions of that boundary are reported as single numbers between 1.17 and 1.98 depending on the configuration; the paper is a 3-page empirical note, not a theorem, and it says nothing about the loss landscape over weights being fractal.

### Cited Findings
- Claim: "this boundary is fractal over more than ten decades of scale in all tested configurations"; the analogy drawn is with the Mandelbrot and Julia sets, where iterating a function and marking parameter values whose iterates stay bounded vs diverge produces a fractal boundary — [Sohl-Dickstein 2024, arXiv:2402.06184 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.06184)
- Network: one hidden layer (two weight matrices W0, W1), input width = hidden width = n = 16, scalar output, mean-squared-error loss; weights, data and labels drawn from a standard normal; mean-field parameterisation (α1 = 1/n, α0 = √(2/n) for tanh/ReLU, √(1/n) for identity); dataset of n² + n = 272 examples (= number of free parameters) for nonlinear nets, n = 16 examples for the deep-linear case — [Sohl-Dickstein 2024, HTML full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06184)
- Training: full-batch steepest gradient descent, 500 steps (sometimes 1000), float64; initialisation and data held fixed across the grid — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06184)
- Hyperparameter plane: learning rate of the input layer (η0) vs learning rate of the output layer (η1) in five of six conditions; the sixth plane is mean of the parameter initialisation vs a single shared learning rate — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06184)
- Six conditions: tanh full-batch (baseline), ReLU full-batch, identity ("deep linear") full-batch, tanh minibatch of size 16, tanh with a single data point, tanh init-mean-vs-learning-rate grid — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06184)
- Convergence/divergence rendering: red = diverged, intensity ∝ sum over steps of 1/loss; blue = converged, intensity ∝ sum of loss over steps; paler = faster convergence or divergence; colour scale adjusted per image — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06184)
- Fractal dimension estimator: box counting (PoreSpy `boxcount`), on ~50 zoom images of 4096×4096 pixels, each a 2× zoom of the previous; reported value = median over the zoom sequence — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06184)
- Measured box-counting dimensions (one number each, no error bars): deep linear full-batch 1.17; ReLU full-batch 1.20; tanh single datapoint 1.41; tanh minibatch 1.55; tanh full-batch 1.66; parameter-initialisation grid 1.98 — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06184)
- Caveats stated by the author: regions to zoom into were chosen by hand ("ad hoc", acknowledged bias); no Lyapunov exponents computed; the minibatch result is only "suggestive of" Lyapunov fractals (the iterated map changes each step); when η0 is very small only the readout trains, which reduces to linear regression with closed-form dynamics and is not fractal; the fractals are "more organic" and less symmetric than Mandelbrot/Julia because the iterated map is high-dimensional with random terms; good hyperparameters tend to sit near the edge of instability, which the author links to chaotic meta-loss landscapes in meta-learning — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06184)
- The arXiv listing gives the paper as 3 pages — [arXiv abstract page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.06184)

### Inferences
- The result is about the map "hyperparameters → (bounded | unbounded) iterate sequence", i.e. about the stability boundary of an iterated nonlinear map. It is a property of the training dynamics as a dynamical system, not a statement that the loss surface L(θ) over weights has fractal geometry. A paper that wants "fractal" to describe the landscape itself cannot borrow this result for that purpose.
- The dimensions (1.17–1.98) are for a curve in a 2-D plane; the author only speculates that the structure extends to higher-dimensional hyperparameter spaces.
- The networks are toy-scale (16 wide, 272 examples, 500 steps, full batch, MSE). Nothing at CNN/transformer scale is measured in this paper.

### Gaps
- No error bars, no scaling-range plots per condition, and no statement of what "ten decades" is measured in (presumably learning-rate magnitude across the zoom sequence) beyond the abstract sentence. The HTML rendition was read; the PDF exceeded the fetch size limit, so the figures were not inspected directly.
- Whether the paper has been peer-reviewed or published beyond arXiv was not determined.

## Q2. Is "SGD = Langevin dynamics at temperature ∝ lr / batch" a theorem or an approximation, and under which assumptions?

### Takeaway
It is an approximation derived under four explicit assumptions (Gaussian gradient noise, constant noise covariance, small-step continuous-time limit, quadratic loss near a minimum) in Mandt–Hoffman–Blei; within those assumptions the stationary distribution is a Gaussian whose covariance is proportional to learning rate and inversely proportional to batch size, which is the "temperature" statement. Smith & Le and Jastrzębski et al. use the same SDE picture to define a noise scale g ≈ εN/B and argue it sets the width of the minima reached; all three papers describe it as an SDE approximation, none as a theorem about exact SGD, and Mandt et al. say outright that the assumptions "are often violated" in the early search phase.

### Cited Findings
- Mandt, Hoffman, Blei, "Stochastic Gradient Descent as Approximate Bayesian Inference", JMLR 18(134):1–35, 2017: "Stochastic Gradient Descent with a constant learning rate (constant SGD) simulates a Markov chain with a stationary distribution" — [JMLR page](https://jmlr.org/papers/v18/17-214.html)
- Section 3.2, "SGD as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process": "To justify the approximation, we make four assumptions. We verify its accuracy empirically in Section 7." Assumption 1: by the central limit theorem over the S minibatch samples, gradient noise is Gaussian with covariance (1/S)·C(θ). Assumption 2: C(θ) ≈ C = BBᵀ constant, "justified when the iterates of SGD are confined to a small enough region around a local optimum". Assumption 3: the finite-difference equation Δθ = −εg(θ) + (ε/√S)BΔW can be replaced by the SDE dθ = −εg(θ)dt + (ε/√S)B dW, "justified if either the gradients or the learning rates are small enough that the discretization error becomes negligible". Assumption 4: the loss is quadratic, L(θ) = ½θᵀAθ with A positive definite (A = Hessian at the optimum) — [JMLR PDF, pp. 6–7](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume18/17-214/17-214.pdf)
- Result: the four assumptions give a multivariate OU process dθ = −εAθ dt + (ε/√S)B dW with Gaussian stationary distribution q(θ) ∝ exp(−½θᵀΣ⁻¹θ), where ΣA + AΣ = (ε/S)BBᵀ; "the resulting covariance Σ is proportional to the learning rate ε and inversely proportional to the magnitude of A and minibatch size S" — [JMLR PDF, p. 7](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume18/17-214/17-214.pdf)
- "Discussion of Assumptions 1–4": three regimes; in the early "search phase ... assumptions 1–4 are often violated and it is hard to say anything general"; the formalism is meant for the phase near a local optimum where the objective "already looks quadratic"; they also assume C has full rank — [JMLR PDF, p. 7](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume18/17-214/17-214.pdf)
- Smith & Le, "A Bayesian Perspective on Generalization and Stochastic Gradient Descent" (ICLR 2018, arXiv:1710.06451): interpreting SGD as an SDE, they define the noise scale g = ε(N/B − 1) ≈ εN/B (ε learning rate, N training-set size, B batch size), implying an optimal batch size B_opt ∝ εN; they argue the Bayesian evidence penalises sharp minima and that minibatch noise drives SGD toward high-evidence (broad) minima — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06451)
- Jastrzębski et al., "Three Factors Influencing Minima in SGD" (arXiv:1711.04623): investigating "the previously proposed approximation of SGD by a stochastic differential equation", the three factors are learning rate, batch size and gradient covariance; "higher values of the ratio [learning rate / batch size] lead to wider minima and often better generalization"; learning-rate schedules can be replaced by batch-size schedules — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.04623)
- Smith, Kindermans, Ying, Le, "Don't Decay the Learning Rate, Increase the Batch Size" (ICLR 2018, arXiv:1711.00489): increasing batch size during training produces the same training and test curves as decaying the learning rate, for SGD, momentum, Nesterov and Adam; ResNet-50 to 76.1 % ImageNet validation accuracy in under 30 minutes — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00489)
- Welling & Teh, "Bayesian Learning via Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics" (ICML 2011): adding Gaussian noise of variance ε_t to an SGD step makes the iterates converge to posterior samples "as we anneal the stepsize" — [ICML 2011 paper](https://icml.cc/imls/conferences/2011/papers/398_icmlpaper.pdf)

### Inferences
- The honest phrasing is: "under a quadratic, constant-covariance, small-step approximation, constant-step SGD behaves like an OU process whose stationary covariance scales as ε/S". The word "temperature" is a physics gloss on that covariance; it is exact only for the linearised model.
- Decaying the learning rate therefore lowers the effective noise scale, which is what justifies calling learning-rate schedules "annealing" (Welling & Teh use the word for the step size; Smith et al. make the equivalence with batch-size growth empirical). This is an analogy supported by the SDE picture, not a convergence guarantee of the simulated-annealing kind.
- Gong et al. (Q3) find heavy-tailed (α-stable) step distributions during training, which directly violates Assumption 1 (Gaussian noise) in the early phase — consistent with Mandt et al.'s own caveat.

### Gaps
- No source found that states "SGD is Langevin dynamics" as a theorem for non-quadratic losses; all treatments found are approximations or asymptotic (small ε) statements.
- The explicit "learning-rate decay = simulated annealing" sentence was not found verbatim in a primary abstract; Smith & Le's full text may contain it, but only abstracts were fetched for that paper.

## Q3. Rugged landscapes: Hessian spectra, edge of stability, annealing, and any measured fractal dimension / Hurst exponent of landscapes or SGD trajectories

### Takeaway
The best-established structural facts are (i) a Hessian spectrum with a bulk near zero plus a small number of outliers tied to the number of classes (Sagun; Papyan: C outliers, C(C−1) mini-bulk), (ii) full-batch GD's top eigenvalue rising to and hovering at 2/η (Cohen et al.; the SGD analogue uses "batch sharpness"), and (iii) two papers that do measure fractal quantities of SGD paths: Gong et al. (anomalous diffusion; path fractal dimension 1.32–2.67, self-affine not self-similar) and Tan et al. (fractional Brownian motion model; Hausdorff dimension of ResNet-20 trajectories ≈ 2.7–3.1). None of these measures a fractal dimension of the loss surface itself at scale.

### Cited Findings
Hessian spectra
- Sagun, Evci, Güney, Dauphin, Bottou 2017 (arXiv:1706.04454): the spectrum "is composed of two parts: (1) the bulk centered near zero, (2) and outliers away from the bulk"; adding parameters with data fixed "merely scales the bulk"; changing the data "only affects the outliers"; flatness "can be measured by the singularity of the Hessian" — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04454)
- Ghorbani, Krishnan, Xiao 2019 (arXiv:1901.10159): full-spectrum tracking during training; "in non-batch normalized networks, we observe the rapid appearance of large isolated eigenvalues"; the gradient concentrates in their eigenspaces; "In batch normalized networks, these two effects are almost absent"; spectrum estimation scaled to ImageNet — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.10159)
- Papyan 2019 (arXiv:1901.08244): the outliers come from the gradient class means (an "additive two-way structure"); the "covariance of gradients" is really a second-moment matrix — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08244)
- Papyan 2020, "Traces of Class/Cross-Class Structure Pervade Deep Learning Spectra", JMLR 21(252): earlier work by Sagun et al. found the number of Hessian outliers "is often equal to the number of classes C"; the three-level hierarchy yields one isolated outlier, C − 1 secondary outliers, a "mini-bulk" of C(C − 1) ≈ C² outliers, and a main bulk; knockouts attribute the C outliers to the between-class covariance G_class and the mini-bulk to the cross-class covariance G_cross; verified on VGG11 across MNIST and CIFAR10 (train and test) with Lanczos spectrum estimation — [JMLR PDF](https://jmlr2020.csail.mit.edu/papers/volume21/20-933/20-933.pdf); [JMLR page](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v21/20-933.html)

Edge of stability
- Cohen, Kaur, Li, Kolter, Talwalkar, "Gradient Descent on Neural Networks Typically Occurs at the Edge of Stability" (ICLR 2021, arXiv:2103.00065): for "full-batch gradient descent on neural network training objectives", "the maximum eigenvalue of the training loss Hessian hovers just above the numerical value 2 / (step size)"; loss is non-monotone over short timescales but decreases over long ones — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00065)
- Cohen et al. note that for SGD the sharpness does not flatline but is "heavily influenced by the step size and batch size" (quoted in later work) — [secondary: arXiv:2412.20553 and related](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.20553)
- Andreyev & Beneventano, "Edge of Stochastic Stability" (arXiv:2412.20553): for minibatch SGD the quantity that saturates at 2/η is "batch sharpness", the expected directional curvature of minibatch Hessians along their stochastic gradients; full-batch λ_max stays below 2/η, consistent with small batches / large steps finding flatter minima — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20553)

Simulated annealing
- Kirkpatrick, Gelatt, Vecchi, "Optimization by Simulated Annealing", Science 220(4598):671–680, 1983, doi:10.1126/science.220.4598.671; Metropolis acceptance of uphill moves with probability exp(−Δe/T) under a decreasing temperature schedule; independently Černý 1985 (J. Optim. Theory Appl. 45:41–51) — [citation verified via Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulated_annealing) (Science.org returned 403; abstract wording not verified)

Fractal dimension / Hurst exponent of SGD trajectories
- Gong et al., "Anomalous diffusion dynamics of learning in deep neural networks" (arXiv:2009.10588, v2 2021): SGD "initially ... exhibits anomalous superdiffusion, which attenuates gradually and changes to subdiffusion at long times"; insensitive to batch size and learning rate; attributed to "the fractal-like structure of the loss landscape" — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.10588)
- Same paper, full text: baseline ResNet-14, batch 1024, CIFAR-10, cross-entropy, lr 0.1 (also MNIST, ResNet-20/56, VGG-like); MSD exponent α > 1 (superdiffusive) early, α = 0.78 (subdiffusive) late; path fractal dimension from Δr² ∼ Δs^λ, D_f = 2/λ ∈ [1.32, 2.67] with two scaling regimes separated by a crossover; transverse distances do not scale with end-to-end distances, so the path is "self-affine rather than self-similar"; step-size distributions fit α-stable laws with stability parameter α_dist ≈ 1.47 early and ≈ 1.9 later (heavy tails); the "fractal-like landscape" test is a mean-squared-loss-difference vs distance power law with exponent 1.8 on scales Δr ∈ [0.4, 10] early in training, flattening later; a toy 2-D fractal landscape (fractal dimension in [1, 2]) reproduces the super→sub transition — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.10588)
- Tan et al., "Trajectory-dependent Generalization Bounds for Deep Neural Networks via Fractional Brownian Motion" (arXiv:2206.04359): SGD modelled as Euler–Maruyama discretisation of dw = −μ dt + σ dΓ with Γ a d-dimensional fractional Brownian motion with per-coordinate Hurst exponents H_i; the hypothesis set explored is "essentially fractal-like"; the Hausdorff dimension of the trajectory image is dim_H Γ([0,1]) = (1 + Σ_{i≤k}(H_k − H_i))/H_k (Xiao 1995), used as an upper bound on the hypothesis-set dimension; per-coordinate H estimated from stochastic-gradient-noise series with the `nolds` library; measured Hausdorff dimensions for ResNet-20 on CIFAR-10 subsets: 3.12 ± 0.07 (1000 examples/class) decreasing to 2.70 ± 0.06 (4000/class), stable across initialisation, subset and minibatch order; experiments also on ResNet and ViT on ImageNet-1K — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.04359); [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04359)
- arXiv:2503.22478 argues "SGD is effectively diffusion on a fractal landscape" and treats the fractal dimension inside a Bayesian framework (SGD as a modified Bayesian sampler with accessibility constraints); the abstract reports experimental verification via weight diffusion but no numbers — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.22478)

### Inferences
- "Rugged" in the Hessian literature means a few sharp class-structured directions over a near-flat bulk, not a scale-invariant roughness; power-law or heavy-tailed claims are about the Hessian spectrum's bulk shape and about SGD's step distribution (Gong: α-stable with α_dist 1.5–1.9), not about a fractal landscape.
- The only direct measurements of fractal geometry are of the trajectory (Gong: D_f 1.32–2.67, self-affine; Tan: Hausdorff ≈ 2.7–3.1 via an fBM model) plus Gong's loss-increment power law over one decade of distance. That is thin evidence for "the landscape is fractal" and should be presented as suggestive.
- Edge of stability gives a clean, reproducible sense in which the "boundary of trainability" is where good training happens; it connects naturally to Sohl-Dickstein's remark that good hyperparameters sit near the fractal boundary.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that estimates a Hurst exponent of SGD parameter trajectories by rescaled-range or DFA directly and reports the number; Tan et al. estimate per-coordinate H from gradient noise but print a histogram, not values. Gong et al. compute a path fractal dimension, not H.
- No paper found measuring a box-counting / correlation dimension of a loss surface L(θ) at realistic scale; Gong's loss-increment power law is the closest.
- Papyan's exact datasets/architectures beyond VGG11 on MNIST/CIFAR10 were not enumerated from the full text.
- Sagun's "power-law spectrum" claim: no primary source stating a power-law Hessian bulk was retrieved; Martin & Mahoney's heavy-tailed weight-spectrum work was not consulted (outside the assigned list).

## Q4. For each fast method: complexity before/after and the one structural assumption that makes it work

### Takeaway
Every asymptotic speedup in this family buys its gain from a specific, provable regularity: far-field smoothness (FMM, H-matrices), smooth error on coarse grids (multigrid), nested dilation-by-2 subspaces (wavelets), factorability of the DFT matrix into log N sparse factors (FFT, butterflies), and complementary low-rank structure (butterfly factorisation, Monarch). None of them speeds up an arbitrary computation; each is a theorem conditional on the structure being present.

### Cited Findings
- Fast multipole method. Greengard & Rokhlin, "A fast algorithm for particle simulations", J. Comput. Phys. 73(2):325–348, 1987, doi:10.1016/0021-9991(87)90140-9. Abstract (as indexed): computing all pairwise Coulombic/gravitational interactions among N particles conventionally costs O(N²); the method reduces this to work proportional to N while accounting for all interactions "to within roundoff error" — [search index of the abstract](https://www.math.pku.edu.cn/teachers/litj/notes/numer_anal/FMM_Greengard_Rokhlin.pdf) (PDF is a scan; text not extractable). Structural assumption: the far field of a distant cluster is approximated by a truncated series expansion (multipole / polynomial), with error controlled by the number of terms p and the hierarchy of well-separated boxes; a Chebyshev-interpolation presentation gives error 5^(−p) and total cost O(N log(1/ε)) — [Wikipedia FMM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_multipole_method). Named among the top ten algorithms of the 20th century (Cipra, SIAM News, May 2000) — [same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_multipole_method)
- Hierarchical (H-) matrices. Hackbusch, "A sparse matrix arithmetic based on H-matrices. Part I", Computing 62(2):89–108, 1999, doi:10.1007/s006070050015. Storage O(n k log n); multiplication, inversion and LU each O(n k² log² n) (k = rank parameter setting accuracy), vs dense O(n²) storage and O(n³) inversion. Structural assumption: for a "sufficiently smooth" kernel, index blocks t × s away from the diagonal can be approximated by rank-k matrices (local low-rank approximation of submatrices; polynomial interpolation of the kernel) — [Wikipedia Hierarchical matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hierarchical_matrix) (Springer page redirected to login; abstract not read directly)
- Multigrid. Brandt, "Multi-Level Adaptive Solutions to Boundary-Value Problems", Math. Comp. 31(138):333–390, 1977 (precedent: Fedorenko 1961). Cost "proportional to the number of unknowns", i.e. O(N), derived as W_1 < K N_1 /(1 − ρ) for coarsening ratio ρ < 1; W-cycle in 1-D is O(N log N). Structural assumption: relaxation (Gauss–Seidel) damps short-wavelength error quickly, leaving a smooth error that is corrected globally on a coarser grid — [Wikipedia Multigrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multigrid_method); [AMS citation](https://www.ams.org/journals/mcom/1977-31-138/S0025-5718-1977-0431719-X/)
- Fast wavelet transform. Mallat, "A Theory for Multiresolution Signal Decomposition: The Wavelet Representation", IEEE TPAMI 11(7):674–693, 1989 (IEEE document 192463). Structural assumption: a finitely generated orthogonal multiresolution analysis — a signal projected onto nested subspaces, each level a two-channel filter bank (low-pass → approximation, high-pass → detail) followed by downsampling by 2 — [Wikipedia Fast wavelet transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_wavelet_transform). O(N) total work follows from the halving (N + N/2 + N/4 + … = 2N filter applications); this sum is standard but was not read from a primary page (see Gaps).
- FFT. Cooley & Tukey, "An Algorithm for the Machine Calculation of Complex Fourier Series", Math. Comp. 19:297–301, 1965, doi:10.1090/S0025-5718-1965-0178586-1. The abstract describes multiplying an N-vector by an N × N matrix "which can be factored into m sparse matrices, where m is proportional to log N", giving N log N instead of N² operations; most useful when N is highly composite — [search summary citing the abstract](https://www.ece.ucdavis.edu/~bbaas/281/papers/CooleyTukey.1965.pdf); precedent Danielson & Lanczos 1942 "doubling trick" — [EE history page](https://ethw.org/Milestones:First_Demonstration_of_the_Fast_Fourier_Transform_%28FFT%29,_1964). Structural assumption: the DFT matrix factors recursively into sparse butterfly stages because of the periodicity/symmetry of the roots of unity.
- Butterfly factorisation. Li, Yang, Martin, Ho, Ying, "Butterfly Factorization", SIAM Multiscale Model. Simul. 2015 (arXiv:1502.01379): an N × N matrix with the "complementary low-rank property" (submatrices pairing a node at one tree level with a node at the complementary level are numerically low-rank) factors into O(log N) sparse matrices each with O(N) nonzeros, so it can be applied in O(N log N) — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.01379)
- Monarch. Dao et al., "Monarch: Expressive Structured Matrices for Efficient and Accurate Training", ICML 2022 (arXiv:2204.00595): for n = m², M = P L Pᵀ R with L, R block-diagonal (m blocks of m × m) and P a fixed reshape-transpose permutation; 2n√n parameters; matvec O(n√n) FLOPs — more than a butterfly's O(n log n) but mapped to batched GEMMs so faster in practice; the Monarch class strictly contains butterfly matrices (merge the log₂ n butterfly factors into two block-diagonal ones); products MM* represent convolution, Hadamard, Toeplitz; (MM*)² represents the Fourier transform, DCT/DST, Fastfood, ACDC; dense→Monarch projection has an analytical optimum despite non-convexity — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.00595). Reported: 2× faster GPT-2 pretraining on OpenWebText with no quality drop; 2× faster ViT/GPT-2 end-to-end sparse training; BERT pretraining 23 % faster than the MLPerf 1.1 record implementation; GLUE fine-tuning 1.7× faster — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.00595)
- FlashAttention. Dao, Fu, Ermon, Rudra, Ré, NeurIPS 2022 (arXiv:2205.14135): exact attention made IO-aware by tiling so the N × N attention matrix is never written to HBM; fewer HBM accesses than standard attention and optimal for a range of SRAM sizes; 15 % end-to-end speedup on BERT-large (seq 512) vs the MLPerf 1.1 record, 3× on GPT-2 (seq 1K), 2.4× on Long-Range Arena (1K–4K) — [NeurIPS page](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/67d57c32e20fd0a7a302cb81d36e40d5-Abstract-Conference.html); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14135). Note: this is a constant-factor memory-hierarchy win, not an asymptotic one — FLOPs stay O(N²).
- Self-similar geometry by instancing. The Menger sponge is the attractor of an IFS of 20 affine contractions of ratio 1/3 in R³, with Hausdorff dimension log 20 / log 3 ≈ 2.727 — [arXiv:1807.08416 (collection of theorems)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.08416); [Wikipedia Menger sponge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menger_sponge). So the entire object at any resolution is specified by 20 transforms (plus the recursion depth), and level-k detail costs 20^k instances rather than stored geometry.

### Inferences
- Per method, the "one assumption": FMM — the kernel is smooth away from the source so far-field clusters compress to p terms; H-matrix — off-diagonal blocks are numerically low-rank; multigrid — the error left after relaxation is smooth, hence representable on a coarser grid; FWT — nested subspaces related by dilation by 2; FFT — the transform matrix factors into log N sparse stages (composite N); butterfly/Monarch — complementary low-rank structure, or, for Monarch, the willingness to replace a dense weight by a two-factor block-diagonal parameterisation; IFS instancing — exact self-similarity.
- Monarch and FlashAttention are the closest to "fractal speedups inside neural networks", but neither claims anything about the loss landscape: Monarch is a structured-weight parameterisation (O(n^{3/2}) matvec) and FlashAttention is a memory-traffic optimisation with unchanged FLOPs.
- The common pattern — hierarchical decomposition with a bounded amount of information crossing each scale — is exactly what is absent in generic dense matrices and in generic non-convex loss surfaces; the speedups do not transfer by analogy.

### Gaps
- Original abstracts of Greengard–Rokhlin 1987, Hackbusch 1999 and Mallat 1989 were not read (403 / login / scanned PDF); page numbers and DOIs are from secondary indexes. One search index gave the FMM page range as 315–348; the standard citation is 325–348 — flag until checked against the journal.
- The FMM operation count formula from the original paper (in terms of N and p) and the exact well-separation ratio were not verified.
- Mallat's O(N) complexity statement was not read from the paper; it is the standard textbook consequence of the dyadic filter bank.
- Kaleidoscope matrices (Dao et al. 2020) and hierarchical attention variants (e.g. H-Transformer) were not researched.

## Q5. Fractal image compression: ratios, limits, why it lost to wavelets/JPEG2000, and what it still shows

### Takeaway
Jacquin's 1992 partitioned-IFS coder targeted 0.5–1.0 bit/pixel on monochrome images (roughly 8–16:1), with secondary sources reporting JPEG-like quality up to about 50:1 and higher ratios on satellite imagery; its decisive weakness was encoding time, dominated by searching a large domain-block pool for each range block, and Davis (1998) showed that Jacquin-style block coders are mathematically Haar-wavelet subtree quantisers — so wavelet coders subsume the idea with far cheaper encoding. What survives is the collage theorem: a small "collage error" under a contractive map bounds the attractor error by 1/(1 − s), which is the rigorous core of "describe a shape by its own self-maps".

### Cited Findings
- Barnsley & Sloan's first IFS patent (US 4,941,193) was filed October 1987; Barnsley's student Jacquin implemented the first automatic algorithm in 1992; Iterated Systems Inc. (founded 1987) licensed the technology to Microsoft for Encarta and received >20 further patents — [Wikipedia Fractal compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractal_compression)
- Jacquin, "Image coding based on a fractal theory of iterated contractive image transformations", IEEE Trans. Image Processing 1(1):18–30, 1992 (IEEE document 128028): "fractal block coding" exploits "self-transformability on a blockwise basis"; the fractal code is a transformation that, iterated from any starting image, converges to an approximation of the original; aimed at "rates in the range of 0.5–1.0 b/pixel" on monochrome images, performing comparably to state-of-the-art vector quantizers — [Konstanz fractal-coding bibliography, abstract](https://uni-konstanz.de/mmsp/fractal2/html/Publi/Jacq92.html)
- Reported ratios (secondary): results similar to JPEG up to about 50:1, possibly superior at high ratios; satellite imagery over 170:1 "with acceptable results"; fractal video 25:1–244:1 at 2.4–66 s per frame — [Wikipedia Fractal compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractal_compression)
- Encoding cost: "encoding is extremely computationally expensive because of the search used to find the self-similarities"; a brute-force domain search for every range block; decoding is fast; one shipped video product was judged "a simple quadtree block-copying system with neither the bandwidth efficiency nor PSNR quality of traditional DCT-based codecs"; TMMI abandoned the technology in January 2016 — [same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractal_compression)
- Search cost scales as O(n²) in an n × n image for the exhaustive domain search, which motivated classifying domain blocks to prune the search — [US patent 5,850,262 citing Jacquin](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5850262)
- Resolution independence ("fractal scaling"): decoding at a higher resolution yields plausible detail but "cannot recover information lost to entropy" — [Wikipedia Fractal compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractal_compression)
- Davis, "A wavelet-based analysis of fractal image compression", IEEE Trans. Image Processing 7(2):141–154, 1998: fractal block coders "of the form introduced by Jacquin (1992) are Haar wavelet subtree quantization schemes"; generalising to smooth wavelets gives an unconditionally convergent scheme with fast decoding, and "fractal coders derive much of their effectiveness from their ability to efficiently represent wavelet zero trees" — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18267389); [Konstanz bibliography](https://uni-konstanz.de/mmsp/fractal2/html/Publi/Davi95a.html)
- Collage theorem (Barnsley, Fractals Everywhere, 1988; first IFS proof in Barnsley, Ervin, Hardin, Lancaster, PNAS 83:1975–1977): for a complete metric space, compact L and an IFS with contractivity s < 1, if the Hausdorff distance h(L, ∪ w_n(L)) ≤ ε then h(L, A) ≤ ε/(1 − s) where A is the attractor — [Wikipedia Collage theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collage_theorem); strict-inequality form restated in [arXiv:2505.09560](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.09560)

### Inferences
- "Why it lost": three compounding reasons supported above — (1) asymmetric cost, an O(n²)-ish search per image on the encode side vs trivial decode, (2) no rate–distortion advantage at practical ratios once JPEG and then wavelet zero-tree coders existed, and (3) Davis's result showing the fractal coder is a special (Haar, hence blocky) case of wavelet subtree coding, so the better-conditioned wavelet bases dominate it. Direct fractal-vs-JPEG2000 benchmark numbers were not found (see Gaps).
- What it still shows: natural images have enough approximate block self-similarity across one octave of scale that a contractive self-map plus the collage theorem reconstructs them at ~10:1; the collage theorem is the clean statement that self-similarity can be *fitted* (an inverse problem) with a controlled error amplification 1/(1 − s).

### Gaps
- Jacquin's own PSNR / compression-ratio tables, block sizes (commonly cited 8×8 range, 16×16 domain) and encoding times were not read; IEEE Xplore returned 403. The 0.5–1.0 bpp figure is from the abstract only.
- No primary source with a side-by-side fractal vs JPEG2000 rate–distortion comparison was found; the claim that wavelets won on rate–distortion rests on Davis's structural result and Wikipedia's qualitative statements.
- The PNAS collage-theorem paper's year appears as 1985 in one secondary source and PNAS vol. 83 is 1986; verify before citing.

## Q6. What this means for the paper

### Takeaway
A paper titled "Fractal optimization of tensor landscapes" can honestly claim, on the landscape side, that the *trainability boundary in hyperparameter space* is empirically fractal (box-counting dimension 1.2–2.0 in small MLPs, Sohl-Dickstein 2024), that SGD paths show anomalous diffusion with self-affine fractal geometry (D_f 1.3–2.7, Gong et al.; Hausdorff ≈ 2.7–3.1 under an fBM model, Tan et al.), and that constant-step SGD behaves like Langevin dynamics with effective temperature ∝ learning rate / batch size *under a quadratic, Gaussian-noise, small-step approximation* (Mandt et al.), which licenses "learning-rate schedule as annealing" as an analogy. On the speed side it can claim that every known asymptotic speedup from scale separation (FMM O(N²)→O(N), H-matrices O(n³)→O(nk² log² n), multigrid O(N), FWT O(N), FFT O(N log N), butterfly O(N log N), Monarch O(n²)→O(n^{3/2})) is conditional on a specific provable regularity, and that self-similar geometry is cheap to instance (Menger sponge = 20 transforms). The one sentence NOT supported by any source found: "recursion (or self-similarity) makes arbitrary shapes or arbitrary optimization faster" — no fast method above works without its structural assumption, no paper measures a fractal dimension of a realistic loss surface itself, and the Sohl-Dickstein fractal is a property of the training map's stability boundary, not of the loss landscape over weights.

### Cited Findings
- Sohl-Dickstein's object is the hyperparameter boundary, dimensions 1.17–1.98, one-hidden-layer width-16 nets — [arXiv:2402.06184](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06184)
- SGD–OU equivalence requires Assumptions 1–4 and "are often violated" early — [Mandt et al. JMLR](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume18/17-214/17-214.pdf)
- Measured trajectory fractal dimensions: [Gong et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.10588); [Tan et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.04359)
- Each fast method's structural precondition: FMM far-field expansion — [Wikipedia FMM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_multipole_method); low-rank blocks — [Wikipedia H-matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hierarchical_matrix); smooth coarse-grid error — [Wikipedia Multigrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multigrid_method); complementary low-rank — [Butterfly factorization](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.01379); Monarch parameterisation — [arXiv:2204.00595](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.00595)
- Fractal compression's encoding-search bottleneck and Haar-subtree equivalence — [Wikipedia Fractal compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractal_compression); [Davis 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18267389)

### Inferences
- Safe framing for the paper: "fractal" describes (a) the stability boundary of the training map in hyperparameter space and (b) the roughness of optimizer trajectories; "speed" comes from exploiting known scale separation in the *data or operator* (weights as Monarch/butterfly, operators as H-matrices/multigrid), not from the landscape being fractal.
- The two halves connect only through the annealing analogy (temperature ∝ lr/batch; schedules move the iterate from rough superdiffusive regions toward flat subdiffusive basins), and that connection should be presented as supported-by-approximation, not proven.

### Gaps
- No source links fractal landscape geometry to an asymptotic optimization speedup; if the paper wants that bridge it must be stated as a hypothesis.
